15  Ribbon

How far apart were the lowest and highest values at each date? ribbon draws a filled band from a low boundary to a high one across x: a confidence band, a spread band, the shaded region around a trend. It fills one region, the way an area does. It reads a low value and a high value at each position, the way an interval does. An area fills from the data down to a baseline at zero. A ribbon has no baseline, and both of its edges come from the data, always through a transform. With a category on one axis and density, the same mark draws a violin plot.

15.1 A band from a range

The gapminder_asia table holds five Asian countries: China, India, Indonesia, Japan and South Korea (Korea, Rep. in the table). Each is measured every five years from 1952 to 2007. Did the life expectancies of those five move closer together over the years, or further apart? The gap between the lowest and the highest in each year answers that. A band drawn between those two values shows every year at once, as one shape. The two boundaries come from a transform, exactly as an interval’s two extents do. range reduces y to its minimum and maximum within each x, and the ribbon fills between them. 15.2 A band with a mean line

On its own, a band shows the spread but not the center. A band is usually drawn with a line for the center, such as the mean. You compose the two by layering, not by adding an option to either mark. A ribbon * range for the spread, a line * mean for the trend, sharing one x and y:

data(gapminder_asia) + x(year) + y(life) +
  ribbon * range + style(color = "steelblue", opacity = 0.25) +
  line * mean + style(color = "steelblue", size = 2) +
  y_label("Life expectancy") +
  title("Mean life expectancy, with its min–max band")
(data(gapminder_asia) + x(col.year) + y(col.life) +
  ribbon * range + style(color = "steelblue", opacity = 0.25) +
  line * mean + style(color = "steelblue", size = 2) +
  y_label("Life expectancy") +
  title("Mean life expectancy, with its min–max band"))
data(gapminder_asia) + x(:year) + y(:life) + ribbon * range +
  style(color = "steelblue", opacity = 0.25) + line * mean +
  style(color = "steelblue", size = 2) + y_label("Life expectancy") +
  title("Mean life expectancy, with its min–max band")
plot(data(gapminder_asia), x(col.year), y(col.life), layer(ribbon, range),

  style({ color: "steelblue", size: 2 }), y_label("Life expectancy"),
  title("Mean life expectancy, with its min–max band"))

“Given gapminder Asia: x is year, y is life, a ribbon derived by range and also a line derived by mean.”

Both layers are steelblue. Left at its default, a ribbon is filled almost solid, and a line of the same color is hard to see on it. opacity = 0.25 makes the band pale, so the mean line is easy to see.

Each layer runs its own transform on the same table. mean reduces each year to one point for the line, and range reduces the same year to a low and high pair for the band. This is the most common ribbon plot: a trend with its uncertainty or spread drawn around it. It is two marks the grammar already has, added with +.

15.3 Nested percentile bands

Which values of life expectancy do most countries have in each year? gm_all holds all the gapminder years: every country, in every year, 142 countries in all. A min–max band over it spans the full range of life expectancy in each year, but only the two extreme countries set its edges. Naming two quantiles instead gives a band that contains most of the countries. range(0.1, 0.9) runs from the 10th percentile to the 90th, so it holds the middle 80% of the countries. range(0.25, 0.75) holds the middle half (A band between two quantiles). The pale band contains the middle 80% of countries. The darker one contains the middle half, and the line is the median. This plot shows something the min–max version cannot. The median rose from 45 years to 72, and the 90th percentile from 67 to 80, so the distance between them fell from 22 years to 8.

The lower edges of the two bands do not rise steadily. Both lower edges, the 10th percentile and the 25th, decline between 1992 and 2002 while the median rises throughout. A min–max band cannot show this, because its lower edge is one country.

Each band is one more layer, so a third pair of quantiles adds a third band. Write the widest band first, because a later layer is drawn over an earlier one.

15.4 Why a ribbon needs a transform

A low and a high are two numbers, and a plain y() supplies one. An area closes on zero: its region’s other edge is zero, which the grammar supplies. A ribbon has no such second number. Law 7, the minimum syllable, says a mark draws only with every position it needs. For a ribbon those are an x, a low and a high. Like an interval, it needs a transform that supplies the low and the high. Write a ribbon without one and the engine refuses it, rather than drawing an empty panel. What it refuses shows the message.

Computing the boundaries in a transform also keeps the channel vocabulary small. The alternative, used by ggplot2, would add ymin and ymax as two more channels that only a few marks use. A box plot would then need five such channels, one for each number it summarizes. With a transform instead, the same low and high values can be drawn as a whisker, as two lines, or as a filled band.

A ribbon can take eight transforms that supply the low and the high. Six are the pair transforms: range, confidence, deviation, smooth_band, straight_band and bounds. Transforms explains each one. The other two are density, over a category, and flow. density gives one curve per category, and the ribbon draws that curve and its mirror image as the two boundaries. That is the violin, later in this chapter. flow is shown in Transforms it takes.

15.5 A confidence band

How well is the mean life expectancy known in each year? That is a different question from how far the five countries spread. range shows the full spread. For the uncertainty of the mean, use confidence. It computes a t-interval for the mean at each x, and the ribbon fills between its two boundaries. Here is the 95% band for the same five countries, on the right, beside the min–max band from the start of this chapter:

“Given gapminder Asia: a ribbon derived by range, x is year, y is life, beside a ribbon derived by confidence at 95%, x is year, y is life.”

The confidence band is wide because each mean comes from only five countries. Those five differ by more than fifteen years, so the interval around their mean is wide. A confidence level above 0.95 draws a wider band still.

The two bands are almost the same width. That comes from having only five values, not from the two transforms agreeing. Over the 142 countries of gm_all, the 95% band in 2007 is about 4 years wide, while their range is 43 years. The plots in Transforms it takes show both. What the band means is decided by the transform you name: range draws the observed spread, and confidence draws the uncertainty of the mean. The mark stays the same in both plots, exactly as it does for interval.

15.6 A pre-computed band

range, confidence, deviation, smooth_band and straight_band compute the band from the raw values in the table, at the moment gog draws the plot. Many bands are computed earlier, by the analysis itself. A model’s standard error, a standard error of measurement, a bootstrap interval: each of these is computed before the plot. Each gives a low value and a high one, and those two become two columns in the table. gog draws what you computed, and it does not re-fit the model. The score_band table holds one row per raw score on a test scored from 0 to 20. Each row also holds the expected true score for that raw score, the score a model predicts, and the two ends of a 95% band around it. In this table the expected true score equals the raw score, so a line through it is a diagonal:

bounds is the pair transform that computes nothing. It reshapes the two columns you give it into the same low and high pair range writes. The band needs no y(), because the two columns are the boundaries. This is the one case range, confidence, deviation, smooth_band and straight_band cannot write: a band that was computed outside the plot, before gog ever read the table.

The band takes the same setting, with different values. On a stroke, pattern takes "dashed", "dotted", "dotdash" or "longdash". On a fill, it takes "hatch", "crosshatch", "stripes", "grid" or "dots", and never "dashed". Both also take "solid", which draws no pattern.

15.7 Filled, or two dashed edges

Some plots need the band without a fill. A band starts as a low and high pair, and the mark decides how that pair is drawn. That is the No Exceptions law: a transform computes the same values whichever mark reads it. The same bin gives a bar chart, a frequency polygon and a step histogram. In the same way, the first figure in A band from a range drew the same pairs with three marks: interval as a whisker at each x, line as two lines, and ribbon filled between them. The unfilled band many journals prefer is the line version, which traces the pair’s two boundaries. With style(pattern = ) it becomes the dashed pair of curves those journals often print. line * bounds is one layer that draws both edges, splitting the pair into a low curve and a high one. So you do not write one y() for the low edge and another for the high edge. Two plain line layers can draw two columns you already have. They cannot split a pair that range computes, because that pair never becomes a column you can name. A line draws every pair transform the same way. line * range traces the lowest and the highest values, and line * confidence traces the two ends of a confidence interval. step * bounds draws the same two edges as staircases. All four marks take the same six pair transforms: range, confidence, deviation, smooth_band, straight_band and bounds. Filled or unfilled, solid or dashed, the pair stays the same and only the mark changes, so ribbon needs no option for any of them.

15.8 A fan chart

A forecast for next year is more certain than one for three years ahead, so a forecast is usually published with bands that widen with each year. A fan chart draws several such bands, such as an 80% and a 50% band, one inside the other. 15.10 One region, one fill

The bands above are equally translucent, and each has its own color. Can you map a column to a band’s opacity instead? No, whether the column is a category or a number. On a ribbon, opacity belongs to the layer, not to each band: every band in the layer has the same opacity, exactly as on area. So opacity is a setting here and not a channel. One band cannot be lighter in one year and darker in another, and one continent’s band cannot be lighter than another’s.

color is different, because it belongs to each band. A categorical column gives each band its own color, as in the plot above. One band still has one color, so a continuous column, which would change the color within a band, is refused. What it refuses shows both refusals.

size is refused both ways: a ribbon neither maps it nor sets it. A band’s extent comes entirely from its x values and its two boundaries, so there is no size to choose. To outline the band, layer a line along its boundaries rather than setting an outline on the mark. The plot in What you can set does this with line * bounds.

15.12 The violin: a distribution per category

A range on a category gives two numbers per group, the lowest and the highest. density gives a whole shape per group, and a ribbon can draw that shape. With density, the category is no longer the domain. Each continent is given a slot, the part of the x axis that belongs to one category. density estimates one curve per continent, and the curve runs along the other axis, y. At each life expectancy, the curve’s value is larger when more of the continent’s countries lie near it. The ribbon draws that value as a width across the slot, from left to right, half on each side of the slot’s center line, so the two halves mirror each other. In the bands drawn so far, the width was the distance between the lower and the upper edge, in years of life expectancy. A violin’s width runs across the slot instead. That is the violin plot (Hintze & Nelson, 1998). By default, each continent’s curve is first multiplied by its number of countries, for the reason given in What the width means. 15.12.1 What the width means

A violin’s width at one life expectancy is measured across its slot, from its left edge to its right edge. By default, it is proportional to the estimated number of countries per year of life expectancy around that value. gog draws the widest point in the panel 0.8 of a slot wide, 0.4 on each side of its center line. Every other width is drawn in proportion to it, so widths can be compared between violins. density(reach = ) sets that 0.4, as Letting the ridges overlap shows.

A density curve is an estimated probability density, so the area under the whole curve is 1: the whole group. Its value at one life expectancy is the share of the group’s countries per year of life expectancy there. By default, gog multiplies each continent’s curve by its number of rows, here its number of countries, which turns each share into a number of countries. Africa’s violin, with 52 countries, then covers 26 times the area of Oceania’s, with 2. Without that step, the two would cover the same area.

The number of countries sets a violin’s area. How wide the violin is also depends on its length, from its bottom tip to its top tip, because its area is its average width times its length. The length comes from how far apart the group’s values lie, plus about six years at each end. Compared with ggplot2’s violin explains the six years. Europe’s thirty countries lie within ten years of each other, so its violin is short and wide. Africa’s fifty-two countries span thirty-seven years, so its violin is long, and its widest point is narrower than Europe’s. Oceania’s violin is the shortest, but its area is so small that its widest point is still the narrowest.

15.12.2 Compared with ggplot2’s violin

If you have used ggplot2, you will notice that gog’s violins differ from those geom_violin() draws on the same data. Three choices cause the difference, and gog makes each one on purpose.

First, the ends narrow to a point. A violin is the density curve mirrored, and gog draws the whole curve. The curve runs three bandwidths past each group’s smallest and largest value. The bandwidth is the width of the bell-shaped kernel that density places on each value. No row in the table lies in these tails. Oceania’s two countries lie at 80.2 and 81.2 years, but with the bandwidth of 2.04 years that all five violins share, its violin runs from 74.1 to 87.4. line * density, split by color(continent), draws the same estimate as the violins, so its curves have the same tails. In One transform, four readings, Oceania’s curve also runs from 74.1 to 87.4. geom_violin() stops each violin at its group’s smallest and largest value, so its ends are flat. With trim = FALSE, it draws tails the same way, three bandwidths long.

Second, by default each gog violin’s area is proportional to its row count, as What the width means explains. geom_violin() scales every violin to the same area, as compare = "shape" does in gog.

Third, every violin in a plot is smoothed by one bandwidth, so a difference in shape comes from the data and not from the smoothing (One transform, four readings). geom_violin() gives each violin its own bandwidth.

In gog, only the second choice can be changed to match geom_violin(). The ends always narrow to a point, and every violin shares one bandwidth, whether gog chooses it or you set it with density(bandwidth = ) (Choosing the bandwidth).

15.12.4 Letting the ridges overlap

The ridgeline above keeps every ridge inside its own slot. That comes from how far a shape reaches by default. A ribbon refuses every transform in the Refuses column above. bin, count, proportion, the summaries, the fitted lines and the running total give one number at each value of x, and a band needs two edges. An area fills one value down to the baseline, and a line draws it as a stroke. A ribbon takes no collision modifier: gog never moves a band aside or piles one band on another. Bands that share a place overlap instead, even two violins split by color in one slot, and a ribbon draws each band translucent, so a band behind another still shows, as One band per group explains. jitter moves each point a short way along the categorical axis, and repel moves text labels. partition and cluster lay out rectangles and branches, which zone and path draw. layout places a network, which point, text and edge draw.

15.14 What you can set

A setting changes how the band looks without reading a column, and each mark takes its own. Setting vs mapping shows how a setting differs from mapping a column. These are a ribbon’s settings, with the values each accepts:

Setting Value
style(color = ) any CSS color name or hex
style(opacity = ) 0 to 1
style(pattern = ) solid, hatch, crosshatch, stripes, grid, dots

A ribbon takes area’s list, and for area’s reasons. There is no size, because the band’s shape comes from the two boundaries its transform supplies. There is no border, because the edge is a line traced along the same pair.

One setting this chapter has not shown on a band is a fill pattern. Here is a band with pattern = "grid", and a dashed line along both of its boundaries. The plot uses the score_band table, shown in A pre-computed band:

“Given the score band: x is score, a ribbon from lower to upper, with pattern grid, and also lines from lower to upper, with pattern dashed.”

Both marks read the same bounds(lower, upper) there, exactly as the two ridge layers did, so the edges stay on the band. The two pattern values follow the rule from A pre-computed band: "grid" is a fill texture, and "dashed" a stroke pattern.

The ribbon is written before the line, and the order matters, because a later layer is drawn over an earlier one. Written after its edges, a band covers the inner half of each dashed stroke, and with a ribbon’s default, nearly solid fill, a line inside the band would almost disappear under it.

The grid of every mark and every setting shows which other marks share this list. What a mark maps rather than sets is its row on the grid of marks and channels.

15.15 What it refuses

15.16 In short

A ribbon fills the space between two boundaries, and refuses to draw until a transform supplies them. The transform decides what the band means: the extremes from range, the middle from two quantiles, the mean’s uncertainty from confidence, and a band computed elsewhere from bounds. Over a category, density makes the ribbon a violin. Both edges come from the data, so the band’s shape is never a setting. opacity belongs to the layer, and color to each band.
