13  Interval

How far does each group reach, from its lowest value to its highest? interval draws a whisker for each group: a span from a low value to a high one, with a short line across each end called a cap. When the statistic behind the whisker has a center, such as the mean inside a confidence interval, a dot marks that center. interval is the mark that draws error bars and min–max ranges. A bar measures from a baseline up to a value. An interval has no baseline, so it needs both a low value and a high value before it can draw. Whiskers are vertical when the categories are on x and horizontal when they are on y; see Horizontal whiskers.

The name can mislead. You met range, confidence, deviation and bounds in the Line and Step chapters, and in statistics an interval is something computed, such as a confidence interval. So interval sounds like one more of those transforms. It is not a transform: interval is a mark, and it computes nothing. A transform computes a low and a high, and interval draws a whisker between them, just as line and step draw the same pair as two strokes.

Almost everyone calls this an error bar, and that name does not say what the whisker measures. Three different quantities are drawn as the same picture: the mean plus and minus one standard deviation, the mean plus and minus one standard error, and a confidence interval (CI) for the mean. On the iris data in this chapter, the standard deviation whisker is about three and a half times as long as the 95% confidence interval. Nothing in the drawing says which one it is, and a reader without a caption cannot know.

gog makes the sentence name the statistic instead. interval * deviation spans one standard deviation on each side of the mean. interval * confidence(0.95) spans a 95% confidence interval, and the level is written in the sentence. A standard error has no transform of its own: compute its two ends yourself, and interval * bounds(lower, upper) reads them from two columns. interval * range, drawn first below, spans the lowest value to the highest. The mark is the same whisker in all of them; what differs is the transform, and the transform is written down. That is the law Explicit Over Implicit, applied to one mark. The name error bar never says which statistic was drawn. A written sentence has to say it.

13.1 A range per group

How far do the petal lengths of each species reach, from the shortest to the longest? The iris_flowers table holds 150 iris flowers, 50 of each of three species, measured in centimeters. One whisker per species answers the question, and a whisker needs a low value and a high value to span. Both come from a transform; Why interval needs a transform explains why. range reduces y to its minimum and maximum within each x group, and interval spans between them:

  title("Range of petal length per species"))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by range, x is species, y is petal length.”

interval * range is written the same way as bar * mean. The difference is what the transform returns: mean returns one number per group, and range returns two. So you do not write a low and a high yourself. You write one column, petal_length on y, and the transform computes the low and the high from it. These two values are the extents, and the mark spans them.

range does not belong to interval alone. Three other marks take the same low and high values, and only the shape changes:

Mark derived by range Shape
ribbon * range the band between the two values, filled
line * range the two values as two separate strokes
step * range the same two boundaries, as staircases
interval * range one whisker per group

range is one of the six pair transforms you met in Two boundaries from a pair, which supply a low value and a high value where most transforms supply a single number. The other five are confidence, deviation, smooth_band, straight_band and bounds, and every mark in that table takes all six. smooth_band and straight_band fit a curve of y against x, so they need a continuous column on both axes. With a category on x, as in every interval in this chapter, they are refused.

13.2 The middle half of each group

A minimum and a maximum each come from a single row, so one unusual flower can decide where a whisker ends. To show the middle of a group instead of its extremes, give range two quantiles:

(data(iris_flowers) + interval * range(0.25, 0.75) + x(col.species) + y(col.petal_length) +
  x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Middle half of petal length per species"))

  y(:petal_length) + x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Middle half of petal length per species")

  x(col.species), y(col.petal_length), x_label("Species"),
  y_label("Petal length (cm)"),
  title("Middle half of petal length per species"))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by range from 0.25 to 0.75, x is species, y is petal length.”

This spans the first quartile to the third, which is the interquartile range. range(0.1, 0.9) keeps the middle 80%, and any other pair works the same way. range written with no arguments means range(0, 1), so it still spans from the minimum to the maximum. Transforms explains the two arguments in full.

13.3 Three layers instead of one mark

The two plots above drew one range for each species: the first from the smallest value to the largest, the second only the middle half. A box draws a similar summary in one mark, five numbers (the median, the two quartiles and the two whisker ends), and you cannot remove one of them. Draw the middle half and the full range as two layers, and the median as a third. You can give any of the three layers its own style, drop one, or replace one:

The gray whisker is the full range, the thick blue whisker is the middle half, and the gold dot is the median. The order of the layers decides what stays visible. A later layer is drawn over an earlier one, so the median is written last. Move it to the front of the sentence and the blue whisker draws over it, and almost none of the dot is left. If you have used ggplot2, this is the rule you already know: a geom added later draws on top. The dot is set opaque as well, since a point is slightly translucent by default.

The three layers name the three statistics they draw. A box plot draws similar information and names none of it, and its whiskers end in a different place. The Box chapter draws a box beside these three layers and shows where.

Because each statistic is its own layer, you can change one and leave the others as they are. Widen the blue whisker to range(0.1, 0.9) and the plot reports a different statistic, with the same marks. Drop the gray layer and the y axis is scaled to the blue whisker rather than to the extremes. 13.4 A confidence interval, with a center

How precisely do fifty flowers estimate the average petal length of their species? That is a different question from how far the flowers spread, and it needs a different statistic. range shows the full spread. For the uncertainty of the mean, use confidence. It computes a t-interval for each group’s mean. That is the confidence interval named at the start of this chapter, and it widens when the group is small. confidence also keeps the mean as the center of the span, and the mark draws a dot there. A whisker with a dot at its center is a pointrange.

  • JavaScript
data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(species) + y(petal_length) +
  x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("95% CI of mean petal length")
(data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(col.species) + y(col.petal_length) +
  x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("95% CI of mean petal length"))
data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(:species) +
  y(:petal_length) + x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("95% CI of mean petal length")
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(interval, confidence(0.95)),
  x(col.species), y(col.petal_length), x_label("Species"),
  y_label("Petal length (cm)"), title("95% CI of mean petal length"))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by confidence at 95%, x is species, y is petal length.”

The level is confidence’s only argument: a number greater than 0 and less than 1, so 0.95 rather than 95. It is 0.95 when you leave it out. A higher level draws a longer whisker, so confidence(0.99) is longer than confidence(0.95). The interval is always a t-interval. For a z-interval, a bootstrap interval, or an interval built from a model’s standard errors, compute the low and the high yourself, put them in two columns, and draw them with bounds.

13.5 The spread of the data

How far is a typical flower from the average of its species? That is a question about the spread of the data, not about how well the average is known. The opening of this chapter named three quantities: a standard deviation, a standard error and a confidence interval. confidence draws the confidence interval. The standard deviation has a transform of its own, deviation:

  • Python

data(iris_flowers) + interval * deviation + x(species) + y(petal_length) +
  x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Mean petal length, with one standard deviation")
(data(iris_flowers) + interval * deviation + x(col.species) + y(col.petal_length) +
  x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Mean petal length, with one standard deviation"))
data(iris_flowers) + interval * deviation + x(:species) +
  y(:petal_length) + x_label("Species") + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Mean petal length, with one standard deviation")
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(interval, deviation), x(col.species),
  y(col.petal_length), x_label("Species"), y_label("Petal length (cm)"),
  title("Mean petal length, with one standard deviation"))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by deviation, x is species, y is petal length.”

deviation with no argument reaches one standard deviation on each side of the mean. deviation(2) reaches two, so that whisker is four standard deviations long. Like confidence, it computes a mean as its center, so it draws a pointrange rather than a bare error bar, a whisker with no dot.

The standard error is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the group size. confidence already computes it and draws the interval built from it, which is what a reader of the plot needs, so gog has no transform that draws the standard error alone. gog computes only a few intervals itself, and draws any other interval from two columns you computed before the plot. To draw one standard error on each side of the mean, compute the mean minus the standard error and the mean plus it, put them in two columns, and draw them with bounds.

deviation and confidence look alike and answer different questions. The two plots below both name petal_length on y, so they share that scale, drawn once down the left. Composition explains when two plots share a scale. Here are the two statistics on the same fifty flowers per species:

((data(iris_flowers) + interval * deviation + x(col.species) + y(col.petal_length) +
   y_label("Petal length (cm)") + title("Spread of the data")) |
  (data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence + x(col.species) + y(col.petal_length) +
     title("Uncertainty of the mean")))
(data(iris_flowers) + interval * deviation + x(:species) +
  y(:petal_length) + y_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("Spread of the data")) |
  (data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence + x(:species) +
  y(:petal_length) + title("Uncertainty of the mean"))
beside(plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(interval, deviation),
  x(col.species), y(col.petal_length), y_label("Petal length (cm)"),
  title("Spread of the data")),
  plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(interval, confidence), x(col.species),
  y(col.petal_length), title("Uncertainty of the mean")))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by deviation, x is species, y is petal length, beside intervals derived by confidence, x is species, y is petal length.”

The whiskers on the right are less than a third as long, and the data is identical. The ratio is the same in all three species. The two statistics do not contradict each other. deviation describes how far the flowers are from their average, and measuring more of them does not make that whisker shorter. confidence describes how precisely the average itself is known. More flowers make its whisker shorter: four times as many flowers make it about half as long. So the ratio between the two whiskers comes from the group size. With six or fewer flowers per species, the confidence whisker is the longer one, because a t-interval widens when the group is small.

So choose the statistic by asking which caption you would write under the plot. “A versicolor petal is usually within half a centimeter of its species average” describes the left panel. “The average versicolor petal is 4.26 cm, and its 95% confidence interval reaches 0.13 cm on either side” describes the right one. Reporting one and meaning the other is the mistake the name error bar allows, and the reason gog requires the statistic in the sentence.

13.6 Why interval needs a transform

Every sentence so far has named a transform. A mark needs one when it draws a number that nothing else supplies. A bar needs no transform, because the grammar already supplies its baseline: zero. bar + x(species) + y(petal_length) draws. An interval has no such default. A low and a high are two numbers, and a plain y() supplies one. So the shortest sentence that draws an interval is the mark, its positions, and a pair transform. Law 7, the minimum syllable, says a mark draws only with every position it needs, and an interval needs two numbers where y() gives one. Ask for an interval without a transform and the engine refuses the sentence rather than drawing an empty panel. What it refuses shows that refusal.

A whisker needs a low and a high, and there are two ways to give it them. One is two more channels, one for each: ggplot2 does this with its ymin and ymax aesthetics. But only a few marks would use those two channels, and a box plot would then need five, one for each of its numbers. gog takes the other way: a pair transform computes the two extents, as mean computes one value per group, so the channel vocabulary does not grow. ribbon reads the same low and high values, and box computes its five numbers inside the mark. The pair transform bounds(lower, upper) takes a low and a high you already have as two columns of the table, and Horizontal whiskers draws a coefficient plot with it.

13.7 Horizontal whiskers

Whiskers turn horizontal for the reasons bars do: the names are long, or there are many of them. A tall list of horizontal whiskers is then easier to read than a crowded row of vertical ones. An interval takes its orientation from the bindings, the way a bar and a box do. Put the categories on y and the measured column on x:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(petal_length) + y(species) +
  x_label("Petal length (cm)") + y_label("Species") +
  title("The same 95% CI, horizontal")
(data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(col.petal_length) + y(col.species) +
  x_label("Petal length (cm)") + y_label("Species") +
  title("The same 95% CI, horizontal"))
data(iris_flowers) + interval * confidence(0.95) + x(:petal_length) +
  y(:species) + x_label("Petal length (cm)") + y_label("Species") +
  title("The same 95% CI, horizontal")
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(interval, confidence(0.95)),
  x(col.petal_length), y(col.species), x_label("Petal length (cm)"),
  y_label("Species"), title("The same 95% CI, horizontal"))

“Given the iris flowers: intervals derived by confidence at 95%, x is petal length, y is species.”

The caps turn too, since a cap always crosses the span at its end, and the center dot stays at the center. The sections after this one work the same way on horizontal whiskers. color, dodge, caps, center and pattern, all introduced below, behave the same whichever axis carries the categories.

This is the usual shape for a coefficient plot: one line of the plot for each term of a model, read from top to bottom. The numbers in such a plot are usually computed when the model is fitted, not from raw observations. That is what bounds is for: it reads two columns you already have as the low and the high, and computes nothing. The coefs table was made for this book. It holds one row for each term of a model of log wage, and lo and hi are the ends of each term’s 95% confidence interval. The term column goes on y. bounds supplies the values for the other axis, so there is no x() to write:

  x_label("Effect on log wage") +
  title("A coefficient plot, from bounds you already have")

The two Region whiskers, North and South, run from a negative low to a positive high. Each one includes zero, so at the 95% level each effect could be negative, zero or positive.

An interval needs a number to span, so two categorical axes are refused, as they are on a bar and a box. What it refuses shows that refusal.

13.8 Splitting by color

With color, a reader can see which whisker is which without reading the axis. color splits an interval into one whisker per group, the same categorical split line and bar make. 13.11 Hiding the center dot

Oceania has both the shortest whisker and the longest, from the same two countries. A confidence interval from two values is wide compared with the distance between them, because two values say little about the spread. In 2007 Australia and New Zealand were a year apart, and the confidence interval is thirteen years wide. In 1957 the two countries were 0.07 years apart, and the confidence interval is still nine tenths of a year wide. That is more than twelve times the 1957 range, which showed as a single cap in Splitting by color.

What this plot does not show is the data. Two groups can have the same mean and the same confidence interval and still hold very different values, and a bar reports only the one number. Three layers instead of one mark draws the spread itself, and box draws five numbers at once.

13.14 Transforms it takes

A transform computes new values from the rows, and the mark draws those values instead of the rows. The table lists, by kind, the transforms an interval takes and the ones it refuses:

Summaries — sum, mean, median, max, min, quantile
Fitted lines — smooth, straight
Running total — cumulative
Pairs: a low and a high range ●, confidence ●, deviation ●, smooth_band ●, straight_band ●, bounds ● —
Collision modifiers dodge stack, jitter, repel

The plots below show every transform an interval takes. Within a figure the plots differ mainly in their transform, and the paragraph before each figure names any other change. The six pair transforms are marked ●: an interval cannot draw without one of them, as Why interval needs a transform explains. Apart from dodge, which only sets whiskers side by side, the pair transforms are the only transforms an interval takes. No other mark is limited to them: a ribbon also needs a low and a high at each place, but it takes density and flow as well.

Pairs. The pair transforms give y a low and a high at each value of x, and the interval spans them. range, confidence and deviation reduce the rows at each value of x, so they need a number on y. range gives the smallest and the largest value. confidence gives the 95% confidence interval of the mean, and deviation reaches one standard deviation from the mean on each side. Both also compute the mean, which the interval marks with a dot. smooth_band and straight_band need a number on each axis. They fit a line to the rows, each placed at its x and its y, and give a low and a high around it at evenly spaced places along x, 100 unless you set places. The interval draws a whisker at each place. bounds computes nothing: it reads the low and the high from two columns you name, and it needs no y. 13.17 In short

An interval computes nothing. Its transform decides which two numbers it spans, and whether there is a center to draw. caps and center are settings, so neither reads a column. A pattern can be set for the whole layer, or mapped to a column, which splits the whiskers the way color does. size and opacity map a column only with bounds, which draws one whisker from each row of your table.
