12  Step

How do you draw a value that holds until it jumps? step draws it as a staircase. A line slopes straight from one point to the next. A step holds each value until it changes, then jumps. The flat part runs from one observation to the next, and the jump is where the value changes. That is the right shape for anything that does not change between observations: a cumulative count, a survival probability, an interest rate. step takes the same channels as line, and only the path drawn between two points differs.

Several professions draw this shape, each under its own name, and step draws each of these plots from a table that holds its values. Statisticians draw the empirical cumulative distribution function (the empirical CDF) with it. Medical researchers draw the survival curve, the Kaplan–Meier estimate (Kaplan & Meier, 1958), which drops at each observed death. Reliability engineers draw the same staircase for parts rather than patients, and call it a failure plot. Analysts in finance draw the step chart of a rate that holds until someone changes it. A cumulative count rises and a survival probability falls, and step draws both as this one staircase.

12.1 A step function

What share of European countries have a life expectancy at or below 75 years, or 80, or any value you choose? The empirical CDF answers all of those questions at once. It is a step function: flat between observations, with a jump at each one. The gm_europe_cdf table holds one row for each of the 30 European countries in 2007: its life expectancy, and the share of countries at or below it. Each country’s share is its rank by life expectancy divided by 30: the lowest has 1/30, about 0.033, and the highest has 1. The table already holds the shares, so step needs no transform: give it an x and a y, and it draws the curve:

The step is exact: between two countries no country is added, so the share does not change.

The cumulative transform computes the same shares from the countries themselves, in three steps. On a continuous axis, count tallies the rows at each distinct value. cumulative adds those tallies up from left to right, so at 75 years the running total is 8 countries. share = TRUE then divides each running total by the last one, the 30 countries. So 8 becomes 8/30, about 0.27, and the curve ends at 1. Without share = TRUE, the axis would count countries, from 0 to 30. The gm_europe table holds the same 30 countries, with no share column:

12.2 A value that holds until it changes

A step is also the right mark for anything that jumps rather than changing gradually: a policy rate (the interest rate a central bank sets), a price, an inventory level. Each holds its value, then jumps on a single day. A straight line would draw a gradual change that never happened. The policy_rates table holds one interest rate for each year from 2018 to 2023. The rate fell to 0.25 in 2020 and held there for two full years, then rose twice. The line slopes into and out of that flat part. Halfway through 2019 it shows 1.25%, a rate that was never set, and it starts rising in 2021, a year before the rate did.

12.3 Multiple series

The policy rate was a single series, one set of rows drawn as one staircase, and often you need to compare two. The inventory table holds the units in stock at two sites, North and South, for each of eight weeks. Only a delivery or a sale changes a site’s stock. To compare the sites week by week, you want both on one plot. color and group split a step exactly as they split a line: one staircase per category. color also gives each staircase its own color and a legend entry; group splits without either. Here two series hold and jump independently:

12.4 A category on the domain

Every x so far has been a number. How does the mean life expectancy compare across continents? A step will draw that plot, but a bar is the better mark for it, as the step drawn below shows. A step reads one axis as its domain, the axis it runs along, and the other as its measure, the quantity it traces. A category is always the domain, so here the domain is x. A category on the domain gets one slot per value, an equal stretch of the axis for each continent, as in Line. The staircase holds each continent’s mean from the center of its slot to the center of the next one:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + step * mean + x(continent) + y(life) +
  y_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + x(continent): the five means as a staircase")
(data(gapminder_2007) + step * mean + x(col.continent) + y(col.life) +
  y_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + x(continent): the five means as a staircase"))
data(gapminder_2007) + step * mean + x(:continent) + y(:life) +
  y_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + x(continent): the five means as a staircase")
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(step, mean), x(col.continent),
  y(col.life), y_label("Mean life expectancy"),
  title("step * mean + x(continent): the five means as a staircase"))

“Given gapminder 2007: a step outline derived by mean, x is continent, y is life.”

Africa is lowest at 54.8 years and Oceania highest at 80.7. Oceania, the last slot, has no next slot, so its mean is only the top of the last jump. The five slots run in the order the continents first appear in the table. That order means nothing, so the rise and fall across the slots is not a trend.

Every flat part of a step is at one of the five means. A line slopes from one category to the next, so between them it passes through heights that are no continent’s mean.

That does not make it the right mark for this plot. A flat part claims that the value held across the part of the axis under it. A policy rate does hold between two dates, as A value that holds until it changes showed. Between two continents there is no value to hold, so the flat part there says nothing about the data. One summary value per category is what a bar draws: keep the mean and change the mark. Each bar is in its own slot, joined to nothing.

Other marks take the same mean, and only the shape changes:

Mark derived by mean Shape
bar * mean one bar per category, each in its own slot
point * mean one dot at each mean
line * mean the means joined into a profile
area * mean the same profile, filled to the baseline
step * mean each mean held flat until the next category, the plot above

gog draws this step and prints no warning. It never refuses a sentence only because another mark would show the data better, so the choice of mark is yours (Law 8).

A staircase can also turn, as the line’s profile does, which gives long category names room down the left side. With the continents on y, the staircase is read down the page:

  x_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + y(continent): the staircase, turned")
(data(gapminder_2007) + step * mean + x(col.life) + y(col.continent) +
  x_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + y(continent): the staircase, turned"))
data(gapminder_2007) + step * mean + x(:life) + y(:continent) +
  x_label("Mean life expectancy") +
  title("step * mean + y(continent): the staircase, turned")

  title("step * mean + y(continent): the staircase, turned"))

“Given gapminder 2007: a step outline derived by mean, x is life, y is continent.”

Each mean is now held from its slot down to the next one, so the parts that hold a mean are vertical and the jumps are horizontal. Oceania, the last slot, again has no next slot, so its mean is only the end of the last jump.

12.5 Histogram outline

mean is not the only transform a step takes. With bin, the staircase answers a question that filled bars make harder to read: how do three distributions compare in one panel? The iris_flowers table holds measurements of 150 iris flowers, 50 of each of three species. Filled histograms of the three overlap wherever two species share a bin, as the Bar chapter’s Overlaid histograms section shows. gog fades those fills so the shapes underneath still show, but the faded colors mix where they overlap. A step has no fill at all, so nothing needs fading. step * bin draws a histogram as an outline rather than as bars: one staircase along the tops of the bins. Add color and the outline splits, one staircase per group, cut on the same bins:

The same bin works with other marks, and only the mark changes:

Mark derived by bin Shape
bar * bin filled bars
line * bin frequency polygon: one stroke of straight pieces through the bin centers
area * bin the same polygon, filled to the baseline
step * bin the staircase outline above

12.6 Stroke width and opacity

The three outlines above are thin, and where two of them run along the same bin edge, the one drawn last covers the other. A thicker stroke is easier to see, and a little transparency lets the covered stroke show through. Like line, a step is a single stroke per series, so on a step size and opacity cannot be mapped to a column; they are settings for the whole stroke:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(petal_length) + color(species) +
  style(size = 2.5, opacity = 0.7) +
  x_label("Petal length (cm)") + title("A thicker, more transparent outline")
(data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(col.petal_length) + color(col.species) +
  style(size = 2.5, opacity = 0.7) +
  x_label("Petal length (cm)") + title("A thicker, more transparent outline"))
data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(:petal_length) + color(:species) +
  style(size = 2.5, opacity = 0.7) + x_label("Petal length (cm)") +
  title("A thicker, more transparent outline")
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(step, bin), x(col.petal_length),
  color(col.species), style({ size: 2.5, opacity: 0.7 }),
  x_label("Petal length (cm)"),
  title("A thicker, more transparent outline"))

“Given the iris flowers: step outlines derived by bin, x is petal length, color by species, with size 2.5 and opacity 0.7.”

size sets the stroke width. opacity makes the whole staircase more transparent. Both are set, never mapped, exactly as on a line; Setting vs mapping explains the difference.

A mapped color is different: it is the one property that can change along a single staircase. A width and a stroke pattern (solid, dashed, dotted and others) belong to the whole stroke. A stroke pattern can still change from one series to the next, as it does for a line, but never along one series. A color belongs to each place on the stroke instead, so a column can change it from one row to the next. Each flat part takes its row’s color, and each jump blends the colors of the two rows it joins. The gapminder_asia table holds five Asian countries, measured every five years from 1952 to 2007:

  color(gdp, scale = "log") + palette("viridis") +

(data(gapminder_asia) + step + x(col.year) + y(col.life) + group(col.country) +
  color(col.gdp, scale = "log") + palette("viridis") +
  y_label("Life expectancy") + title("Each country's staircase, colored by its income"))
data(gapminder_asia) + step + x(:year) + y(:life) + group(:country) +

“Given gapminder Asia: step outlines, x is year, y is life, grouped by country, color by gdp on a log scale, with the viridis palette.”

scale = "log" makes equal ratios of income equal steps of color: each tenfold rise in income moves the color by the same amount. palette() chooses the colors a mapping uses. "viridis" is a ramp, and the named palettes and what each is for are in Palettes.

Read one country’s staircase from left to right and you see two variables. The height is how long people live, and the color is how rich the country was then. The color stays the same along each flat part and changes only at the jumps, where the value changes too.

12.7 Transforms it takes

A transform computes new values from the rows, and the mark draws those values instead of the rows. The table lists, by kind, the transforms a step takes and the ones it refuses:

Kind Takes Refuses
Counting bin, count, density, proportion —
Summaries sum, mean, median, max, min, quantile —
Fitted lines smooth, straight —
Running total cumulative —
Pairs: a low and a high range, confidence, deviation, smooth_band, straight_band, bounds —
Collision modifiers — dodge, stack, jitter, repel
Layouts of a whole picture — partition, flow, layout, cluster

The plots below show every transform a step takes. Within a figure the plots differ mainly in their transform, and the paragraph before each figure names any other change.

Counting. count and proportion tally the rows at each value of x, on a category or a number. bin and density work on a number. bin counts the rows in each interval, and density estimates, as a smooth curve, how closely the rows lie along the axis. Each puts its result on the axis you leave empty. The top two plots put continent on x, and the plot below them puts life. With continent on x, the staircase holds each count from the center of one slot to the center of the next. A category on the domain draws the same shape with mean. step * bin draws the same kind of outline as Histogram outline:

A step refuses every transform in the Refuses column above. These are the same transforms a line refuses. The collision modifiers move marks that would be drawn on top of one another, such as bar and point. A step is a thin stroke, so two staircases that meet cross instead. A step is also read by its height, and a stack would raise each staircase by the ones below it, so its height would no longer be its own value. To pile one series on another, use stack with area, whose filled bands show what each series adds. repel moves only text labels. partition, flow and cluster lay out rectangles, bands and branches, which zone, ribbon and path draw. layout places a network, which point, text and edge draw.

The staircase and the dots both seem to change the line, but they change different things. The staircase decides where the line goes: it puts a corner wherever the value changes. The dots decide only how the line looks, never where it goes. That is why step is a mark, while a stroke pattern only changes how a mark is drawn.

The grid of every mark and every setting shows which other marks share this list. What a mark maps rather than sets is its row on the grid of marks and channels.

12.9 What it refuses

A step refuses four kinds of sentence: a mapped size or opacity, a fill texture, a category on both axes, and a split that leaves one position in every series. A step is one connected stroke with no filled interior, and the first two refusals follow from that. The third comes from the value a step traces, and the fourth from a series that holds a single position. A step also refuses two more sentences a line refuses, for the same reasons: a turned profile in polar(), and a step drawn over horizontal bars. The Line chapter shows both.

The fourth is shared by line, area, ribbon and path. A staircase needs at least two positions in each series. A plot in which every series holds one would draw nothing, so gog refuses it, as Line shows.

12.10 In short

A step holds each value until it changes, so between two observations it draws no value that was not in the data. It takes the same channels and settings as a line: width and opacity belong to the whole stroke, and only color varies along it. With color, group or a mapped pattern it draws one staircase per category, and with bin it draws a histogram as an outline instead of as bars.

12.11 What comes next

Each staircase has one value at each x. Interval spans two values at every position instead, from a low one to a high one.
