22  Scales

What do you do when most of your data is crowded into one corner? You change the scale, which decides how a number becomes a position. It is written on the binding, not as an atom of its own:

x(gdp, scale = "log")

A scale belongs to one channel, so it is written where that channel is bound. There is no scale() atom to write separately. A separate atom would make you name the channel twice, once to bind it and once to scale it, and the two could then disagree.

Writing the scale on the binding also makes a sentence easier to read. To see how gdp is placed, you look at x(gdp, scale = "log") and nowhere else. Nothing else in the sentence can change it, and there is no second function to look up.

22.1 A scale is not a transform

A log scale is not the only way to see a spread of incomes. You could take the logarithm of the column yourself and plot the result. That is a different operation, and the difference decides what the axis says.

bin and mean are transforms: they read your table and compute new values from it. A scale produces no new values at all. It changes how far along the axis a number lands and what the ticks say, and nothing else.

The difference is visible in the finished plot. Here is gdp plotted with a log scale:

gapminder_2007: first 5 of 142 rows
country continent year life population gdp
Afghanistan Asia 2007 43.828 31889923 974.5803
Albania Europe 2007 76.423 3600523 5937.0295
Algeria Africa 2007 72.301 33333216 6223.3675
Angola Africa 2007 42.731 12420476 4797.2313
Argentina Americas 2007 75.320 40301927 12779.3796
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp, scale = "log") + y(life) +
  color(continent) +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Income and life expectancy: log scale")
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp, scale = "log") + y(col.life) +
  color(col.continent) +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Income and life expectancy: log scale"))
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp, scale = "log") + y(:life) +
  color(:continent) + x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Income and life expectancy: log scale")
plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp, { scale: "log" }),
  y(col.life), color(col.continent), x_label("GDP per person"),
  title("Income and life expectancy: log scale"))

“Given gapminder 2007: points, x is gdp on a log scale, y is life, color by continent.”

The axis reads 1K and 10K. Those are dollars, the units you supplied.

The word log appears nowhere on the axis, and that is deliberate. The units did not change, so the axis label does not claim they did. The ticks are the only sign: each is a whole power of ten, so equal steps along the axis mean equal ratios. Write x_label("GDP per person (log scale)") when a reader needs the scale named in words. The axis label is yours, and gog never writes the scale into it.

Now the same picture, drawn after taking the logarithm of the column yourself:

gm_logged <- gapminder_2007
gm_logged$log_gdp <- log10(gm_logged$gdp)

data(gm_logged) + point + x(log_gdp) + y(life) +
  color(continent) +
  title("The same shape, with the axis in log units")

Every point is in the same place as in the first plot, but the axis now reads 2.5 to 4.5. Those numbers are logarithms, not dollars. The tick at 3.0 sits where the first plot has 1K, because ten to the power of 3 is 1,000. The tick at 3.5 means ten to the power of 3.5. That is about 3,162 dollars, a number most readers need a calculator to find. A log scale does that calculation for the reader: its ticks are already in dollars.

So the choice between the two is about what the quantity is. Take the logarithm of the column when that logarithm is itself the quantity of interest: a log-odds, a decibel, a pH. Use a log scale when the quantity is still dollars or people, and you only want to see it across orders of magnitude.

22.2 The order of a scale and a transform

Once a transform is in the plot, a scale is no longer only about display, because a transform has to choose which numbers to work on. gog follows one rule, and it has no per-transform exceptions:

A transform groups by one axis and measures on the other. The scale is applied before the transform on the grouping axis, and after it on the measured axis.

22.2.1 Scaling the grouping axis

A histogram of income is the first plot where that rule changes the result. Its bins have to be cut somewhere, either in dollars or in the logarithms of those dollars.

bin groups by x, so a log x is applied first. The bins are cut on the logarithms, not on the dollars. Each bin then spans one constant ratio. Every bar is therefore the same width, and the bars fill the axis from end to end:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(gdp, scale = "log") +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Bins cut in log space are evenly spaced")
(data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(col.gdp, scale = "log") +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Bins cut in log space are evenly spaced"))
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(:gdp, scale = "log") +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("Bins cut in log space are evenly spaced")
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(bar, bin), x(col.gdp, { scale: "log" }),
  x_label("GDP per person"),
  title("Bins cut in log space are evenly spaced"))

“Given gapminder 2007: bars derived by bin, x is gdp on a log scale.”

Suppose the bins had been cut in dollars and only the drawing had been logarithmic. The first bin alone would cover most of the axis, and the rest would crowd into the right edge. That reads as a mistake rather than as a modeling choice, which is why the grouping axis is scaled first.

22.2.2 The measured value keeps its own units

Put the log on the measured axis instead and a different question follows. Does a bar on a log scale stand for a sum of dollars, or for a sum of logarithms?

sum writes to y, so a log y is applied after the sum. The two bars in this plot stand for 100 and 10:

receipts: all 4 rows
store sales
North 10
North 90
South 1
South 9
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(receipts) + bar * sum + x(store) + y(sales, scale = "log") +
  title("A sum is still a sum")
(data(receipts) + bar * sum + x(col.store) + y(col.sales, scale = "log") +
  title("A sum is still a sum"))
data(receipts) + bar * sum + x(:store) + y(:sales, scale = "log") +
  title("A sum is still a sum")
plot(data(receipts), layer(bar, sum), x(col.store),
  y(col.sales, { scale: "log" }), title("A sum is still a sum"))

“Given the receipts: bars derived by sum, x is store, y is sales on a log scale.”

If the scale had been applied first, gog would have added the logarithms, giving the log of a product, which is not a quantity anyone asked for. gog adds the dollars first and scales the total afterwards, so sum reports a sum of dollars.

If you have used ggplot2, note the order, because ggplot2 does the opposite. Under scale_y_log10(), a stat_summary(fun = mean) averages the logarithms of the values. The result is a geometric mean, the average computed on the logarithms. gog computes the summary in your data’s units and scales the result, so the number the bar stands for is the number you would get by taking the sum or the mean yourself.

22.2.3 smooth: a curve on a log axis

The grouping axis raises the same question for smooth: is the curve fitted to dollars, or to their logarithms? On incomes that run from a few hundred dollars to nearly fifty thousand, the answer changes the shape of the curve.

smooth groups by x, so with a log x the curve is fitted against log income. Life expectancy rises by about the same amount for each tenfold rise in income, so the curve is close to a straight line:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp, scale = "log") + y(life) +
  style(color = "lightgray") +
  line * smooth + x(gdp, scale = "log") + y(life) +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("A trend fitted against log income")
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp, scale = "log") + y(col.life) +
  style(color = "lightgray") +
  line * smooth + x(col.gdp, scale = "log") + y(col.life) +
  x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("A trend fitted against log income"))
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp, scale = "log") + y(:life) +
  style(color = "lightgray") + line * smooth + x(:gdp, scale = "log") +
  y(:life) + x_label("GDP per person") +
  title("A trend fitted against log income")
plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp, { scale: "log" }),
  y(col.life), style({ color: "lightgray" }), layer(line, smooth),
  x(col.gdp, { scale: "log" }), y(col.life), x_label("GDP per person"),
  title("A trend fitted against log income"))

“Given gapminder 2007: points, x is gdp on a log scale, y is life, and also a line derived by smooth, x is gdp on a log scale, y is life.”

22.3 What a log scale refuses

Some columns cannot take a log scale, and they are not rare: a depth that goes below zero, a balance that went negative, a count of zero.

A logarithm is undefined at zero and below, so rows with such values have no position on the axis. gog says so rather than dropping them quietly:

depth temp
1 12
0 14
-4 15
100 9

22.4 Choosing the base

Not every quantity is best read in powers of ten. Ten is the usual base for money and for population, but a pitch doubles with every octave, and a decay is counted in factors of e.

scale = "log" means base 10 unless you say otherwise:

x(freq, scale = "log", base = 2)

The base is a number, not a name. There is no "log2" or "ln" scale. One base argument already covers every base, so a separate name for each would add words without adding meaning. The same objection rules out ggplot2’s scale_x_log10 family.

22.4.1 The base changes the labels, not the picture

You might expect a new base to move the points. It does not move a single one. Any two logarithms of the same column differ by a constant factor, and gog normalizes every axis by its own range. The factor cancels, so the points land in the same places in every base. The transforms are unaffected too: bins of equal width in base 2 are equal width in base 10, so bar * bin cuts at the same values whichever base you name.

What the base actually chooses is where the gridlines fall and how they read. Here are six frequencies, each one an octave above the one before it, on base 10 and then base 2:

“Given the octaves: points, x is freq on a log scale in base 2, y is level.”

The points have not moved. The second plot puts a gridline on each doubling, which is what “an octave” means. So for pitch, bits, or doubling times, base 2 is the one whose labels name what you are counting.

22.4.2 Natural log

The natural base is for a quantity that decays at a constant rate. The amounts in the decay table shrink by a factor of e every hour, so there is one gridline between each reading and the next. A change by a factor of e is called an e-folding.

R has no constant named e, so you write base = exp(1). The other three bindings have one: Python’s math.e, Julia’s ℯ, and JavaScript’s Math.E.

decay: first 5 of 6 rows
hours amount
0 100.000000
1 36.787944
2 13.533528
3 4.978707
4 1.831564

data(decay) + point + x(amount, scale = "log", base = exp(1)) + y(hours) +
  x_label("Amount remaining") +
  title("Gridlines at each e-folding: one step is a factor of e")
(data(decay) + point + x(col.amount, scale = "log", base = math.e) + y(col.hours) +
  x_label("Amount remaining") +
  title("Gridlines at each e-folding: one step is a factor of e"))
data(decay) + point + x(:amount, scale = "log", base = ℯ) + y(:hours) +
  x_label("Amount remaining") +
  title("Gridlines at each e-folding: one step is a factor of e")
plot(data(decay), point, x(col.amount, { scale: "log", base: Math.E }),
  y(col.hours), x_label("Amount remaining"),
  title("Gridlines at each e-folding: one step is a factor of e"))

Notice the labels: e, e², e³, e⁴, not 2.718, 7.389, 20.086, 54.598. That follows from one rule gog applies to every base:

Label the quantity when it is a short number; otherwise label the power.

Base 10 gives 1, 10, 100, 1K, 10K and so on, up to 100B. K means a thousand, M a million, and B a billion (10⁹). The letters stop at B, so 10¹² would read 1000B, with four digits before the letter. From 10¹² upward, a tick is labeled as a power instead, such as 10¹². Base 2 gives 1, 2, 4 and so on, up to 8388608. A number with more than seven digits is labeled as a power instead, such as 2²⁴. Base e has no short quantity above 1, so every tick past the first reads as a power, which is what a reader counting e-foldings wants. If instead you want the axis to read 0, 1, 2, 3 in ln units, you do not want a log scale at all. You want log() applied to the column, and an axis labeled in log units, like the log_gdp plot earlier in this chapter. A scale keeps the reader’s units; a column of logarithms changes them.

A base only means something as the base of a logarithm, so gog refuses one that has no log scale to belong to:

  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript

data(octaves) + point + x(:freq, base = 2) + y(:level)
plot(data(octaves), point, x(col.freq, { base: 2 }), y(col.level))

22.5 Time: a date column gets a calendar axis

A column of dates raises a question the other columns do not. Where do the ticks go, and what do they say, when the units are years and months rather than tens and hundreds?

The time scale is one you never have to write. A column of dates or date-times (Date or POSIXct in R) is temporal, because its type already says so. gog reads the calendar from the type, the same way a column of text already gets a categorical axis:

revenue: first 5 of 26 rows
day sales
1994-06-30 52
1995-06-30 57
1996-06-30 55
1997-06-30 61
1998-06-30 64
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(revenue) + line + x(day) + y(sales) +
  title("Twenty-six years, ticked in years")
(data(revenue) + line + x(col.day) + y(col.sales) +
  title("Twenty-six years, ticked in years"))
data(revenue) + line + x(:day) + y(:sales) +
  title("Twenty-six years, ticked in years")
plot(data(revenue), line, x(col.day), y(col.sales),
  title("Twenty-six years, ticked in years"))

The sentence names no scale at all. Writing scale = "time" is allowed and adds nothing, exactly like writing scale = "linear" on a number.

The ticks land on calendar boundaries, because the calendar is not decimal. An axis of years steps 1, 2 or 5 like any number line. For units smaller than a year those steps mean nothing: nobody reads an axis cut into fifths of a year. Months step by 1, 2, 3 or 6, and each of those divides twelve. A gridline therefore always falls on January, and a 3-month step marks each quarter. Days step by 1 or 2, and weeks by 1 or 2, always on Mondays. Hours and minutes step in the units a clock uses.

22.5.1 Bars on a date axis

Daily orders make a natural bar chart, one bar per day, and forty-two bars raise the question of where the ticks go.

Bars positioned at dates are an ordinary time series. The ticks still come from the calendar, and there is not a tick for every bar. Wilkinson’s stock-price example puts its ticks on Sundays rather than on every trading day (Wilkinson, 2005), and gog ticks these six weeks at the start of each week:

  • Python

  • JavaScript

A bar that measures a date is refused. A bar’s length is an amount, and a moment in time is not an amount. A bar “reaching” 2007 would be measured from 1970, the year a stored date counts from, which nobody chose as a baseline:

22.5.2 What a time axis refuses

A date is stored as a number underneath. You might therefore expect a date to take a log scale, and a number to take a time scale. Neither is allowed.

A moment has no logarithm. The calendar’s zero is an arbitrary convention, and a logarithm measured from an arbitrary zero measures nothing:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(revenue) + line + x(day, scale = "log") + y(sales)
data(revenue) + line + x(col.day, scale = "log") + y(col.sales)
data(revenue) + line + x(:day, scale = "log") + y(:sales)
plot(data(revenue), line, x(col.day, { scale: "log" }), y(col.sales))
Error:
! gog: `x(day, scale = "log")` — `day` is a date column, and a moment in time has no logarithm: the calendar's zero is an arbitrary origin. Log the measured axis instead, or remove the scale.
gog: nothing was rendered. Fix the above, or set GOG_STRICT=0 to draw anyway.

gog can read the calendar off a date column, but you cannot declare a plain number temporal at the binding. The engine cannot know whether a number like 20656 counts days, seconds, or years:

  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript

22.7 The axis fits your data

Where should an axis end, on the data or on the next round number past it? Padding to a round number leaves part of the panel blank, and implies that the data reaches further than it does.

An axis shows round numbers on its ticks, but the range follows your data, not the round numbers. Each tick is a round number that falls inside the data, or at most a little past it into the margin. The axis does not extend to the next multiple of ten only to end on a round number. A baseline gets no margin. The area of Asian life expectancies above shows it: the shape starts on the panel’s bottom edge, not a little way above it. 22.9 limits: stating the range yourself

Every rule so far takes the range from the data. That is the right default, but it is wrong when the data does not cover every value the axis should show. The range an axis should cover is its domain, and it is not always the range the data reaches. The tide table holds a gauge’s readings, one height every three hours from hour 1 to hour 22, so none falls at midnight. polar() draws an axis as a circle, and Polar teaches that space in full. Nothing in a column of hours tells that circle that hour 24 is the same place as hour 0. limits states the domain:

  polar(), title("The whole day, whatever hours the gauge recorded"))

“Given the tide: a line, x is hour from 0 to 24, y is height, in polar.”

Without limits, one turn would cover only 1 to 22, and the first and last readings would be drawn on the same spoke. With limits, each hour sits at its share of the day: hour 6 a quarter of the way around, hour 12 halfway. The curve still does not close, and should not. The gauge took no reading at midnight, and a domain says what range the axis covers rather than inventing an observation. The gap is in the readings, and the plot shows it at its true size, the three hours from 22 to 1. Polar describes three cases: two in which the curve closes, and this one, in which it does not.

limits is written on the binding, beside scale and base, for the same reason they are: a domain belongs to one channel, and an atom of its own would name that channel twice. A stated end is drawn exactly where you state it. The margin exists to keep a glyph off the panel edge, and an end you chose is not a data extreme that needs the room.

A domain can widen the axis as easily as it narrows it. Nothing was dropped in that plot: the readings run 1 through 22 and all of them lie inside 0 through 24. Narrowing is the direction that removes rows, and gog reports how many rows it removes:

The count is a target, not an exact number. The count sets the step between ticks, and the step is then rounded to 1, 2 or 5 times a power of ten. So asking for eight ticks on an axis from 0 to 100 gives a step of 10, and eleven ticks. What you can rely on is the direction: a higher count never gives fewer ticks than a lower one. Every tick is a round number. The count also has an upper limit: an axis draws at most 26 ticks. If the rounded step would give more than 26 ticks, the engine uses a larger round step. The axis is still labeled from one end to the other, and a message names both steps.

A log axis and a date axis take a count too, but their steps are rounded to different values. On a log axis the ticks can be at every power of the base, or skip one or more powers between them. In base 10 they can also be at 1, 2 and 5 times each power. A date axis uses calendar steps, such as a day, a week, a month or a year. The time section describes these steps. On these two axes, the engine picks the step whose number of ticks is closest to your count.

A legend is not an axis, and it takes no tick count. limits reaches every channel that measures, because every one of them has a domain; tick_count reaches only the three position channels that draw an axis: x, y and z. A color legend names three rows, both ends and the middle, and the scale, linear or log, sets their values, not a count you choose. The middle row is the midpoint explained above: the label for the color painted halfway along the ramp. So gog refuses a tick count on color(), size() or opacity(), the same way it refuses scale and limits on shape(): there is nothing for the count to describe.

22.11.1 Crowding in the plane and in the cube

A flat panel and a cube follow one rule for crowded numbers: no number may be printed over another. A flat continuous axis draws every tick it chose, as long as no two numbers overlap. Where panels are side by side, an end number that would reach past its panel is moved inward. In narrow panels, such as the five panels of a plot faceted by continent (Faceting), the numbers can still overlap. The axis then draws fewer numbers, and the ticks and gridlines of the others are left out too. The numbers it keeps are the ones a larger round step would choose, so 0, 10K, 20K, 30K and 40K become 0, 20K and 40K. A number left out can be read from the numbers on either side, so no message is printed. If you stated a tick_count, a message says how many ticks were drawn. A polar plot, a map, and an axis freed with free = TRUE still draw every number they chose.

The sentence below asks for twelve ticks, but each panel has room for three, and a message says so:

“Given gapminder 2007: points, x is gdp with 12 ticks, y is life, split into panel columns by continent.”

A cube can also have too little room for its numbers. In a 3-D plot (Space), each axis is longer or shorter depending on the viewing angle. Turning the cube lengthens one axis and shortens another. The cube then draws as many numbers as fit and leaves the rest out. When a count you stated cannot fit, the engine says so rather than quietly drawing fewer.

Names on a categorical axis follow the same rule: no name may be printed over another. The axis tries to name every category, and a name is usually much wider than a number. When names do not fit side by side, a flat axis first turns them 90 degrees, to read upward. A turned name needs room along the axis only for its height, not its width. When even turned names are too close, the flat axis leaves some out, as the cube does. It draws one name in every two or three, each with its tick, and prints a message. 22.12 axis: leaving an axis out

An axis lets a reader decode a position, as a legend lets a reader decode a color. Sometimes the reader does not need it. On a map, a reader finds a place by its shape and its neighbors, not by its longitude and latitude. Write axis = FALSE on the binding, beside scale and limits, and the axis is left out: its tick marks, its numbers and its name. The map below draws world_borders, which holds one row for each point along a coastline or a border:

22.13 In short

A scale changes where a number lands on the axis. It never changes the number itself. limits states the domain: narrowing it removes rows and reports how many, while a brush removes none. tick_count is a target, not an exact number. A crowded continuous axis, flat or in a cube, draws fewer numbers. A crowded categorical axis turns its names, then leaves some out and says so. axis = FALSE leaves an axis out and keeps its scale.

22.14 What comes next

A scale decides how far along a channel a number lands. It does not decide which visual property shows the column. Channels covers that: which property carries a column, and what guide it gets.
