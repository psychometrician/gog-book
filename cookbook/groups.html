44  Many groups

How do several groups compare? There are two ways to split the picture. You can split the marks inside one panel, with color or group. You can split the plot into panels, with | or /. The recipes below use both, and the last two also put the groups in an order you choose.

44.1 How do the groups’ distributions compare?

The first route is to split the marks. The iris flowers table holds three species, and every row carries a petal length. The question is whether those lengths overlap or sit apart. An overlaid histogram answers it. bin cuts one set of intervals from all the rows, so every species is counted into the same intervals. Each species is drawn as a translucent fill under its own solid outline:

iris_flowers: first 5 of 150 rows
sepal_length sepal_width petal_length species
5.1 3.5 1.4 setosa
4.9 3.0 1.4 setosa
4.7 3.2 1.3 setosa
4.6 3.1 1.5 setosa
5.0 3.6 1.4 setosa
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(iris_flowers) + bar * bin + x(petal_length) + color(species)
data(iris_flowers) + bar * bin + x(col.petal_length) + color(col.species)
data(iris_flowers) + bar * bin + x(:petal_length) + color(:species)
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(bar, bin), x(col.petal_length),
  color(col.species))

“Given the iris flowers: bars derived by bin, x is petal length, color by species.” Setosa sits apart, sharing no bin with the other two. Versicolor and virginica overlap near 5, where their fills blend into a darker band. The solid outline is what keeps each species readable there. Owning chapters: Bar, Transforms, Channels.

44.2 What if the overlap gets worse?

Three translucent fills are still readable. Add more groups, or move them closer together, and the overlaps blend into one dark band where no single fill can be followed. Drop the fills entirely: step * bin draws each group’s outline as an unfilled staircase, and an outline covers only the thin line it draws:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(petal_length) + color(species)
data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(col.petal_length) + color(col.species)
data(iris_flowers) + step * bin + x(:petal_length) + color(:species)
plot(data(iris_flowers), layer(step, bin), x(col.petal_length),
  color(col.species))

“Given the iris flowers: step outlines derived by bin, x is petal length, color by species.”

Only the mark changed, from bar to step. Each group is now one line rather than a filled area, so far less of the plot is covered. Step owns the staircase.

44.3 Grouping without a legend

Both recipes above named their groups with color, and a legend decoded the names. Sometimes the grouping matters and the names do not: you want the shape of many series, not a legend naming each. group divides the rows into one line per country. It binds no color, so there is nothing for a legend to decode:

gapminder_asia: first 5 of 60 rows
country continent year life population gdp
China Asia 1952 44.00000 556263527 400.4486
China Asia 1957 50.54896 637408000 575.9870
China Asia 1962 44.50136 665770000 487.6740
China Asia 1967 58.38112 754550000 612.7057
China Asia 1972 63.11888 862030000 676.9001
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_asia) + line + x(year) + y(life) + group(country)
data(gapminder_asia) + line + x(col.year) + y(col.life) + group(col.country)
data(gapminder_asia) + line + x(:year) + y(:life) + group(:country)
plot(data(gapminder_asia), line, x(col.year), y(col.life),
  group(col.country))

“Given gapminder Asia: lines, x is year, y is life, grouped by country.”

Five countries, five lines, no legend. What you read is the shape the five lines make together, not any one country’s line. Bind color instead when the reader needs to know which is which.

44.4 What if each group needs its own axes?

gapminder 2007 holds five continents, and overlaying them on one histogram would draw five fills on one axis. The first two recipes had only three groups to separate. When each group needs its own pair of axes, split the plot into panels instead of splitting the marks. The bins and the scale stay shared, and each panel counts only its own rows, so the panels can be compared:

gapminder_2007: first 5 of 142 rows
country continent year life population gdp
Afghanistan Asia 2007 43.828 31889923 974.5803
Albania Europe 2007 76.423 3600523 5937.0295
Algeria Africa 2007 72.301 33333216 6223.3675
Angola Africa 2007 42.731 12420476 4797.2313
Argentina Americas 2007 75.320 40301927 12779.3796
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(life) | facet(continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(col.life) | facet(col.continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(:life) | facet(:continent)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(bar, bin), x(col.life),
  across(col.continent))

“Given gapminder 2007: bars derived by bin, x is life, split into panel columns by continent.”

Each panel holds one continent’s distribution of life expectancy. Compare the panels one at a time. Africa’s bars sit at the low end of the axis. Europe’s bars sit at the high end. Bins and the count axis are shared, so equal heights mean equal counts. Faceting owns the rules, including why the cut is shared when the count is not.

44.5 What if two columns group the rows?

Continent is one grouping and year is another. You want each continent drawn in 1957 and again in 2007, to see what changed between the two years. | splits into panel columns, / into panel rows; written together they cross into a grid. Every combination gets a panel. The two years are taken from gm_all, the whole gapminder table. Subsetting is the host language’s job, so it happens in R, and the other three bindings do it their own way:

gm_all: first 5 of 1704 rows
country continent year life population gdp
Afghanistan Asia 1952 28.801 8425333 779.4453
Afghanistan Asia 1957 30.332 9240934 820.8530
Afghanistan Asia 1962 31.997 10267083 853.1007
Afghanistan Asia 1967 34.020 11537966 836.1971
Afghanistan Asia 1972 36.088 13079460 739.9811 
gm_years <- gm_all[gm_all$year %in% c(1957, 2007), ]
gm_years$period <- as.character(gm_years$year)

What follows is the sentence, and it has four spellings like every other:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gm_years) + point + x(gdp, scale = "log") + y(life) |
  facet(continent) / facet(period)
(data(gm_years) + point + x(col.gdp, scale = "log") + y(col.life) |
  facet(col.continent) / facet(col.period))
data(gm_years) + point + x(:gdp, scale = "log") + y(:life) |
  facet(:continent) / facet(:period)
plot(data(gm_years), point, x(col.gdp, { scale: "log" }), y(col.life),
  across(col.continent), down(col.period))

“Given the gm years table: points, x is gdp on a log scale, y is life, split into panel columns by continent and panel rows by period.”

Each column holds one continent, and its two panels are fifty years apart. Read a column downward to see what changed. year is a number, and a facet variable has to name its panels, so period holds the same years as a category. That is what the Over time refusal is about.

44.6 Which group is biggest?

The medals table lists five countries, and the question is which of them won the most gold. A comparison is easiest to read when it is sorted, and a column of plain text carries no order of its own. order() is how you give it one:

medals: all 5 rows
country gold silver bronze
USA 46 37 38
China 38 31 22
Great Britain 29 17 19
Russia 19 18 9
Germany 17 10 15
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(medals) + bar + x(country) + y(gold) + order(gold, desc = TRUE)
data(medals) + bar + x(col.country) + y(col.gold) + order(col.gold, desc = True)
data(medals) + bar + x(:country) + y(:gold) + order(:gold, desc = true)
plot(data(medals), bar, x(col.country), y(col.gold),
  order(col.gold, { desc: true }))

“Given the medals: bars, x is country, y is gold, ordered by gold, largest first.”

One atom away. desc = FALSE, the default, sorts smallest first. order() names gold here, not the category column country. You name the column that holds the values, and the categories are arranged to follow it. It overrides a declared order as well.

44.7 Where does a population pyramid come from?

Two cities each have a population count in every age band, and the question is how the two age structures compare. The chart drawn for that question is the population pyramid. It is a named chart type, and no atom in the kernel carries that name. You never ask for a pyramid; you describe one.

The census table has three columns: an age band, a city (Busan or Seoul), and a population. Each city has 18 bands, so the table holds 36 rows. The numbers are invented; the columns are the ones a census would hold.

census: first 5 of 36 rows
age city population
0 Busan 5200
5 Busan 3100
10 Busan 2600
15 Busan 9000
20 Busan 38500

The mirrored shape comes from one minus sign. Busan’s populations are negated, so Busan’s bars run to the left of zero. That is arithmetic, so it happens in the table, before the sentence:

mirrored <- census
mirrored$population <- ifelse(mirrored$city == "Busan",
                              -mirrored$population, mirrored$population)
mirrored: first 5 of 36 rows
age city population
0 Busan -5200
5 Busan -3100
10 Busan -2600
15 Busan -9000
20 Busan -38500

The arithmetic is written in R, because arithmetic is the host language’s job, and the other three bindings negate a column the same way. What follows is the sentence, and it has four spellings like every other:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(mirrored) + bar + y(age) + x(population) + color(city) +
  order(age, desc = TRUE)
(data(mirrored) + bar + y(col.age) + x(col.population) + color(col.city) +
  order(col.age, desc = True))
data(mirrored) + bar + y(:age) + x(:population) + color(:city) +
  order(:age, desc = true)
plot(data(mirrored), bar, y(col.age), x(col.population), color(col.city),
  order(col.age, { desc: true }))

“Given the mirrored table: bars, y is age, x is population, color by city, ordered by age, largest first.”

No new atom, and none was needed. The pyramid comes from three rules the grammar already states. First, orientation is read off the bindings, so a category on y makes the bars horizontal. Second, a bar runs from the zero baseline to its value, in whichever direction that value lies, so a negative bar is not a special case. It is the same rectangle, pointing the other way. Third, stack is something you ask for, never something the engine does on its own. Nothing here asks for it, so two bars in one slot sit on opposite sides of zero. There is no coord_flip() as in ggplot2, and no barh() as in matplotlib, because either would be a second way to say one thing.

order(age, desc = TRUE) is the atom from the section above, in its other form. There it named gold, a different column from the one on the axis. Here it names the category column itself, so it asks for that column’s own order and reverses it. gog puts the first category at the top when the category is on y, so age 0 would sit at the top. A pyramid needs its youngest band at the bottom, and desc = TRUE reverses the order.

Now look at the left half of the population axis. It reads -40K, not 40K.

Many versions of this chart replace each tick’s text with its absolute value. The minus sign that placed one city’s bars then disappears. gog leaves every tick reading the number it drew at, and this plot shows why. Relabeled, the axis carries two different ticks both reading 40K, and only a reader who knows the convention can tell which is which. That reader never needed the absolute values. The reader who did is left with bars whose positions no longer match the numbers on the axis. gog gives you no way to write your own tick text, and Coverage lists that among the capabilities with no decision recorded. The paragraph above is the reason gog has not added one.

44.8 What this section refuses

shape separates categories on a point, one glyph per category, and you might expect the same on a bar. A bar is a rectangle; it has no glyph to vary, so shape on a bar is refused outright. One table says what every mark accepts, and it has no per-mark exceptions:

  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(medals) + bar + x(country) + y(gold) + shape(country)
data(medals) + bar + x(col.country) + y(col.gold) + shape(col.country)
data(medals) + bar + x(:country) + y(:gold) + shape(:country)
plot(data(medals), bar, x(col.country), y(col.gold), shape(col.country))
Error:
! gog: `shape` cannot be bound to `bar` — a bar has no shape feature. Remove the `country` mapping from `shape`, or use a mark that has one.
