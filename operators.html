26  Operators

How do you put two things in one picture? You join them with an operator, and gog has exactly four. They are not atoms. They are the assembly rules: how atoms combine.

Hangeul (한글) works the same way. Its consonants and vowels are the atoms. Its operator is one rule: an initial consonant, a vowel and an optional final consonant make a syllable block. The rule never changes, and a small set of letters builds every syllable the language needs.

+   layer     (add a layer, a channel, or a label to a specification)
*   derive    (combine a mark with a transform into a compound mark)
|   facet ↔   (small multiples, arranged in columns)
/   facet ↕   (small multiples, arranged in rows)

| and / also arrange whole plots on a page. That second use has a chapter of its own (Composition).

26.1 +: layer

Every sentence you have written so far holds several atoms, and + joins most of them. Each mark, channel, label and setting enters a specification through it:

data(my_table) + point + x(height) + y(weight) + color(species) + title("My plot")

Adding a mark (point, line, bar, …) starts a new layer. A channel (x(), color(), …) written before any mark belongs to the plot, and one written after a mark belongs to that mark. A label atom (title(), x_label()) belongs to the plot wherever you write it (Encoding scope).

26.1.1 Layering with +

A second mark draws a second layer over the first, and video editing works the same way. A video is a stack of tracks: the picture, the sound, the captions. Each track is separate, they play at the same time, and you see one finished video.

“Given the iris flowers: bars derived by bin, x is petal length, color by species.”

Six sentences, and the same histogram six times. The six are not plots that merely look alike. The engine writes six files, identical byte for byte.

So if the order does not change the picture, why does this book use one order: data, mark, positions, refinements? Because a fixed order lets the code read like an English sentence, with a natural, subject-first shape:

  • data(...) first. It names the table the columns come from. A bare name like petal_length means nothing until a table is bound (Bind-Once), so the table is named first. The sentence must start here.
  • The mark and its *-chain second. This is the subject. Nothing is drawn without a mark (Minimum Syllable): bar * mean * dodge says what you are drawing and what was done to it, as one compound word. It is the initial consonant of the block; everything after is said about it.
  • The positions third. x and y say where the subject stands, and a mark cannot be drawn without them (the vowel to the mark’s consonant), so they follow immediately.
  • The refinements last. color, size, order, labels, title: everything optional. Delete every refinement and a legal plot remains; delete the mark or a position and nothing is drawn. Writing the removable parts last is also how you build a plot: the minimum sentence first, then refine (First plot).

Only two parts of the order are rules: the sentence starts with a source (data() or query()), and style() follows its mark. The rest is a reading convention: inside one layer, any arrangement draws the same plot. Where you write a channel does matter once a plot has two layers, because a channel written before a mark and one written after it bind differently. Encoding scope gives the rule. Learn one order and use it everywhere. Then your plots and the plots in this book read the same way.

Which orders matter lists what the engine enforces under + and *, and what you are free to arrange.

26.1.2.1 A refinement far from what it refines

One part of the convention is easy to misread. A grouped bar chart pairs dodge with color, yet the sentence puts them at opposite ends, with the positions in between:

gm_eras: first 5 of 284 rows
country continent year life population gdp era
Afghanistan Asia 1957 30.332 9240934 820.8530 1957
Afghanistan Asia 2007 43.828 31889923 974.5803 2007
Albania Europe 1957 59.280 1476505 1942.2842 1957
Albania Europe 2007 76.423 3600523 5937.0295 2007
Algeria Africa 1957 45.685 10270856 3013.9760 1957
  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gm_eras) + bar * mean * dodge + x(continent) + y(life) + color(era) +
  title("Mean life expectancy by continent, 1957 vs 2007")
(data(gm_eras) + bar * mean * dodge + x(col.continent) + y(col.life) + color(col.era) +
  title("Mean life expectancy by continent, 1957 vs 2007"))
data(gm_eras) + bar * mean * dodge + x(:continent) + y(:life) +
  color(:era) + title("Mean life expectancy by continent, 1957 vs 2007")
plot(data(gm_eras), layer(bar, mean, dodge), x(col.continent),
  y(col.life), color(col.era),
  title("Mean life expectancy by continent, 1957 vs 2007"))

“Given the gapminder eras: bars derived by mean and dodge, x is continent, y is life, color by era.”

dodge and color(era) are plainly related: on a bar, dodge has nothing to set side by side until a channel such as color names the groups. So why not write them together? Because the grammar deliberately gives them different operators. dodge belongs to *, because it modifies the mark. The same modifier works on box * dodge and interval * range * dodge, where group can make the split instead of color. color belongs to +: it binds a column to a channel. color * dodge cannot be written: * needs a mark on its left. Joining the two would make dodge depend on which channel splits the groups, and a modifier must not depend on that.

The order is not a compromise. It follows from what dodge does. dodge subdivides the x slot: each continent’s slot is cut into one bar per era. So dodge uses both atoms: x(continent) says which slot to divide, color(era) says into how many pieces. The sentence lists them in the order dodge uses them: the axis it splits along, then the groups it splits into. The positions do not separate dodge from what it needs; one of them is the slot dodge divides.

You also never have to read back through the sentence to check that the split is there. If no channel splits the mark, bar * mean * dodge is refused rather than drawn as one bar per slot. The message tells you to add a channel that splits it, such as color. So color can stand far from dodge in the sentence and still work with it.

26.2 *: derive

A mark on its own draws the rows as they are. A histogram counts them first, and a trend line fits a curve to them first. That computing step needs a word of its own. * joins a transform to a mark, and the result is one compound mark.

bar * bin     # histogram
line * smooth # smoothed trend line
line * density # kernel density curve

* has one rule and one freedom. The mark goes on the left and the transforms go on the right. Among the transforms themselves, any order draws the same plot. The rest of this section explains both.

26.2.1 Why the order is fixed

Start with the rule. In Hangeul, a syllable is always initial consonant + vowel, never the reverse. gog’s * works the same way: the mark is the subject; the transform is what is done to it.

bin * bar is refused, just as ㅏ (a) + ㄱ (g) is not a valid Hangeul syllable.

bar * bin   # a bar mark, derived by binning

The refusal names the fix:

bin * bar
Error in `*.gog_atom`:
! gog: `*` not defined for transform * mark.
Use `*` to combine a mark with a transform, e.g. bar * bin.

26.2.2 One fixed place, and one free order

The mark stands on the left, always, and no transform may stand there. That is what makes bar * bin a compound mark rather than a function call. Why +, and not a pipe explains why it lets the atoms stay nouns.

Write the transforms in the order they happen. This is a reading convention: any order draws the same plot, and this one lets you follow the steps as you read. bar * bin * mean cuts the axis into bands, then averages inside each one. That is the order it runs in, and there is no other order it could run in.

The reason is what mean needs. A statistic reduces a group to one number, and a continuous column has no groups: every value stands alone, so its mean is itself. Ask for the mean of life at each value of gdp, and each group holds one country:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + point * mean + x(gdp) + y(life)
data(gapminder_2007) + point * mean + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life)
data(gapminder_2007) + point * mean + x(:gdp) + y(:life)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(point, mean), x(col.gdp), y(col.life))
gog: `point * mean` groups the rows by their value of `gdp`, and here every group holds a single row, so each `mean` is one row's own value. Drawn as asked. To summarize, cut `gdp` into ranges first, `point * bin * mean`, or group by a column whose values repeat.

“Given gapminder 2007: points derived by mean, x is gdp, y is life.”

The picture is the plain scatterplot, and the note printed with it says why: each mean is one row’s own value. bin is what makes the groups, by turning many distinct numbers into one band. So the cut comes first: without it, the mean has nothing to average.

So two transforms work together only when they do different jobs. There are four jobs: making the groups, measuring inside them, rescaling the result, and arranging the marks. A pair that does the same job twice is refused rather than run in sequence. bin and count both count the rows, so each cell would be measured twice:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin(12) * count + x(life)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin(12) * count + x(col.life)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin(12) * count + x(:life)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(bar, bin(12), count), x(col.life))
Error:
! gog: `bar * bin * count` measures each cell twice — `bin` and `count` each invent their own measurement from the rows, and neither was handed a column to give way to, so there is no reading that keeps both. Keep whichever you meant: `bar * bin` or `bar * count`. To cut an axis into cells and measure something else inside them, the second transform has to be one you hand a column: `bar * bin * mean + x(<number>) + y(<column>)`. To read either as shares of the whole rather than as counts, `proportion` rescales whichever you keep: `bar * bin * proportion`.
gog: nothing was rendered. Fix the above, or set GOG_STRICT=0 to draw anyway.

The engine decides the running order from the transforms themselves, not from where you typed them. The order you type can never be the wrong order, although the pair itself can still be refused, as the refusal above shows. Chaining transforms shows why a pair is refused, one refusal at a time, and Combinations has the whole grid of pairs.

26.3 | and /: facet, or compose

Two questions lead to the last two operators: how the same plot looks for each group, and how two different plots fit on one page. | puts things side by side and / puts one above the other. What you write on the right decides the job: a facet() splits one plot into panels, and a second plot is placed beside or below the first.

With facet(), | produces small multiples arranged in columns, one panel per category of the column it names.

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) | facet(continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life) | facet(col.continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life) | facet(:continent)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp), y(col.life),
  across(col.continent))

“Given gapminder 2007: points, x is gdp, y is life, split into panel columns by continent.”

/ stacks the panels in rows. Five rows would make each panel too short, so this example uses two continents:

gm_two <- gapminder_2007[gapminder_2007$continent %in% c("Africa", "Europe"), ]
data(gm_two) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) / facet(continent)

“Given the gm two table: points, x is gdp, y is life, split into panel rows by continent.”

Write | and / in one sentence to combine them into a grid. Each operator applies to the facet written after it, read from left to right. See Faceting for the grid, what the panels share, and why the facet column must be categorical.

With a plot on the right, the same two operators arrange separate plots on one page, and each plot keeps its own axes:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp) + y(life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + bar * count + x(continent))
((data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + bar * count + x(col.continent)))
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + bar * count + x(:continent))
beside(plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp), y(col.life)),
  plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(bar, count), x(col.continent)))

“Given gapminder 2007: points, x is gdp, y is life, beside bars derived by count, x is continent.”

These two measure different things on y, life expectancy and a count, so each draws its own vertical axis. When two plots side by side name the same column on y, that axis is shared and drawn once. Two plots one above the other share x the same way.

One pair of operators, two jobs. Composition covers the second.

26.4 Which orders matter

Most of the order data, mark, positions, refinements is a reading convention, and the engine enforces the rest. The difference tells you where to be careful. There are three kinds of ordering, and only one of them needs your attention.

Kind What it means Under + Under *
Free Either way draws exactly the same picture. In a plot with one mark: the mark, its positions and its other channels, in any arrangement. Labels go anywhere. Only style() must follow its mark. Two transforms after one mark. Write them in the order they happen; the engine runs them in that order either way.
Refused The engine stops and names what to move. Atoms with no table named before them: point + x(gdp). A style() written before any mark. A transform on the left: bin * bar.
Meaningful No error, and a different picture. A channel before the marks, or after one of them, once a plot has two. Two marks swapped: the second draws on top. Nothing. No order under * changes the picture.

Start with the cell where * meets Meaningful. * fixes one thing only, which is that the mark stands on its left. Once the mark is there, no arrangement of what follows changes what you get.

Free covers most arrangements. The two sentences below draw the identical plot, and the book writes the first because a fixed order is easier to read:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp) + y(life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + x(gdp) + point + y(life))
((data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + x(col.gdp) + point + y(col.life)))
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life)) |
  (data(gapminder_2007) + x(:gdp) + point + y(:life))
beside(plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp), y(col.life)),
  plot(data(gapminder_2007), x(col.gdp), point, y(col.life)))

Both name life on y, so the two plots share that axis and it is drawn once, on the left. Each plot still draws its own gdp axis underneath it.

Refused means an atom comes before the thing it needs. A plot needs its table first, so + must have a plot on its left. A setting needs a mark, so a style() before any mark is refused:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + style(size = 3) + point + x(gdp) + y(life)
data(gapminder_2007) + style(size = 3) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life)
data(gapminder_2007) + style(size = 3) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), style({ size: 3 }), point, x(col.gdp),
  y(col.life))
Error:
! gog: `style()` has no mark to style. Put it after a mark, e.g. `point + style(color = "tomato")`.

Meaningful is the only kind that needs care, because it is the only kind the engine says nothing about. With two marks in a sentence, a channel written before them belongs to the plot and reaches both, and a channel written after one belongs to that mark. Both are legal, neither warns, and they draw different pictures. That is the subject of Encoding scope. It is also why this book keeps one order: when every sentence looks the same, a different arrangement is a deliberate signal.

The marks themselves are the other case the engine does not report. Two marks swapped draw in the order you wrote them, so the second sits on top of the first.

26.4.1 What groups first

Those three kinds are about where you may put a word. Precedence answers a different question: when a sentence holds more than one operator, which one groups first. Arithmetic has the same idea. 2 + 3 * 4 is 14 and not 20, because * groups before +. It is read as 2 + (3 * 4), as if the parentheses were written. A sentence in gog is read the same way, so precedence decides which atoms each operator joins.

This order of grouping belongs to the language you write in, not to gog, and gog cannot change it. R and Python share one order. Julia differs in one place: it groups | together with +. When the two meet, the one written first groups first. The table gives the order in each of the three languages:

Groups R Python Julia
First * and / * and / * and /
Next + + + and \|, from left to right
Last \| \|

JavaScript has no column in the table, because it spells each operator as a word, such as beside(). The things the word joins are written inside its parentheses, so you see the grouping instead of remembering it (JavaScript).

For one plot, this is the order you want in all three languages. So a plot needs no parentheses of its own, even with a facet:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(life) | facet(continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(col.life) | facet(col.continent)
data(gapminder_2007) + bar * bin + x(:life) | facet(:continent)
plot(data(gapminder_2007), layer(bar, bin), x(col.life),
  across(col.continent))

R reads that sentence as (data(gapminder_2007) + (bar * bin) + x(life)) | facet(continent). The transform joins its mark first, the atoms join the plot next, and the facet splits the finished plot last. Python and Julia read it the same way.

/ groups before +, so a row facet is grouped in a different order. In the row facet drawn earlier, y(life) / facet(continent) is grouped first, and + then joins that pair to the plot. A facet always splits the whole plot, whichever atom it was grouped with. So that sentence draws the same file as (data(gm_two) + point + x(gdp) + y(life)) / facet(continent).

An atom can also be written after a facet, like the title in the sentence below:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
  • JavaScript
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) |
  facet(continent) + title("Each continent in its own panel")
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life) |
  facet(col.continent) + title("Each continent in its own panel"))
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life) | facet(:continent) +
  title("Each continent in its own panel")
plot(data(gapminder_2007), point, x(col.gdp), y(col.life),
  across(col.continent), title("Each continent in its own panel"))

In R and Python, + groups before |, so title() joins the facet first, and then both join the plot. Julia groups | together with +, from left to right, so the facet joins the plot first and the title joins after it. The title appears once, above all five panels, in every language.

26.4.2 Parentheses on a page

That order works for one plot. On a page, it can group a sentence in a way you did not mean. Both sides of | or / should be whole plots, and precedence can group part of one plot with part of the other before both plots are complete. Here are the two plots from earlier in this chapter, set one above the other with no parentheses:

  • R
  • Python
  • Julia
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) /
  data(gapminder_2007) + bar * count + x(continent)
(data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(col.gdp) + y(col.life) /
  data(gapminder_2007) + bar * count + x(col.continent))
data(gapminder_2007) + point + x(:gdp) + y(:life) / data(gapminder_2007) +
  bar * count + x(:continent)
Error:
! gog: `/` places one plot below another, and it groups before `+`, so here it joined `y()` to the plot after it. Put each plot in parentheses: `(data(df) + point + ...) / (data(df) + bar + ...)`.

/ groups before +, so it joins only the two atoms on either side of it: y(life) and the second data(). R reads the sentence as … + x(gdp) + (y(life) / data(gapminder_2007)) + …. A facet can be grouped with one atom, because it splits the whole plot anyway. A plot cannot: / places a whole plot below another, and y(life) is not a plot. So gog refuses the sentence. Its message names y(), the atom that / joined. gog gives the same refusal in Python and Julia.

Written with | instead, the same sentence draws in R and Python. In those two languages, | groups last, so both plots are complete before | joins them. Julia groups | together with +, from left to right. So | joins the first plot to the second plot’s data() alone, and gog refuses the sentence.

So whenever this book composes plots in R, Python or Julia, it writes each plot inside its own parentheses. Then every plot is complete before | or / joins it, and the page means the same thing in all three languages. In JavaScript each plot is written as plot(…), so it is complete already.

Two more places need parentheses. The first is a page inside a page. / groups before |, so a | b / c is read as a | (b / c): one plot beside two plots, one above the other. To put a row of two above a third plot, write (a | b) / c. The second is a theme() for the whole page: put parentheses around the page before you add it. Without them, R and Python give the theme() to the second plot, because + groups before |. Julia gives it to the page (How big is the page itself?).

The table collects the rules for a page. Each (…) holds one whole plot, from its data() to its last atom, and the last column gives the same page in JavaScript’s words:

Page R, Python and Julia JavaScript
Two plots side by side (…) \| (…) beside(plot(…), plot(…))
One plot above another (…) / (…) below(plot(…), plot(…))
A row of two above a third ((…) \| (…)) / (…) below(beside(plot(…), plot(…)), plot(…))
A height for the whole page ((…) \| (…)) + theme(height = 260) beside(plot(…), plot(…), theme({ height: 260 }))

The table above shows what parentheses group: whole plots and whole pages. You might also try them around several marks, to give a second table to all of them. That does not work, because parentheses do not group marks. A second data() applies only to the mark written directly after it (Data):

! gog: parentheses do not group marks, so everything inside these would be dropped. Write the marks in sequence instead, and repeat `data()` before each one that reads that table: `+ data(forecast) + point + data(forecast) + area`. Parentheses compose whole plots, with `|` and `/`.

gog refuses that instead of drawing it. The refusal is better than the alternative. The engine cannot give the second table to both marks inside the parentheses. Its only other choice is to discard both marks and draw a plot with two layers missing, without saying so. To put two marks on the second table, write data() before each of them.

The same refusal covers a position or a title inside the parentheses, for the same reason. In JavaScript, where the operators are words, the same mistake is a plot() handed to another plot(), and it is refused there too.

26.5 Why +, and not a pipe

Readers who know R’s tidyverse packages ask why gog does not use a pipe. A pipe passes a value into a function: a |> f means f(a). Hadley Wickham, ggplot2’s author, has said publicly that the pipe would have been the better interface (Wickham, 2018). ggplot, ggplot2’s predecessor, was written as function composition, and it could have used a pipe if one had existed. ggplot2 used + instead.

He also answers the question a reader asks next: why not change ggplot2 now? It cannot change: its functions take the mapping and the data first, while a pipe needs the plot as the first argument. And he judges that a new package built only to make this change would cost more work than the change would be worth. So ggplot2’s + comes from its history, not a fresh choice, and a grammar designed today has to make that choice for itself. He gives two reasons for preferring the pipe, and neither applies to gog. A third consideration is gog’s rather than his, and it is the one that decides the question.

His first reason is about one language. A pipe is a single idea that works everywhere in R, so a grammar that spells function composition differently is one more thing to learn. Switching between the two spellings is a frequent source of errors, his own included. That is true, and it is an argument about R and its packages. gog is one syntax written in four languages, and that changes the answer. + can be given a meaning in R, Python and Julia. The pipe cannot be given a gog meaning. Julia’s |> and R’s two pipes already mean something gog must not change. Python has no pipe operator at all, and no way to add one: you can give new meanings only to the operators it already has. In R and Julia a pipe would work with the meaning it already has. Python would need another spelling, and the languages would then have two different grammars. This project exists to avoid that. JavaScript decides nothing here, because it cannot overload any operator at all, which is why it spells all four as words (JavaScript).

The second is about arithmetic, and it is the stronger of the two. You expect a + b to equal b + a, and ggplot2’s + does not always do that. That objection is answered by the six permutations drawn earlier in this chapter. Here the mark and the channels commute. Move color ahead of x, or write the positions before the mark, and the engine produces the identical file. One thing does depend on order: two marks swapped. There the order is the drawing order you asked for, and the second mark draws on top.

The other half of that objection is grouping, whether a + (b + c) means what (a + b) + c means. The question cannot arise here, because a + (b + c) cannot be written at all. + always takes a plot on its left, so two atoms with no table named before them never form a smaller expression to group. They are refused:

point + x(gdp)
Error:
! gog: these atoms have no plot to join — the sentence starts with the data: `data(df) + point + x(gdp) + ...`.

The third point is gog’s own, and it is what decides the question. It is not an argument against the pipe. It is a statement of what a pipe would remove. A pipe applies functions, so every atom would have to become a function taking a plot and returning one. Make bar a function, then ask what bar * bin could mean. There is no answer: you cannot multiply two functions and get a histogram. The derive operator exists only because marks and transforms are things rather than actions. They are values, and they combine with each other before either one is joined to a plot.

This is the decision the book opens with: a plot is a specification, not drawing code. ㄱ is a letter, not an instruction. You put it in a slot next to other letters, and the block is assembled from its pieces rather than performed in sequence. Hangeul’s atoms are nouns, which is exactly why they combine, and gog’s are nouns for the same reason. A grammar built on the pipe is a grammar of verbs: it can layer, because layering really is a sequence of additions, but it cannot derive. ggplot, ggplot2’s predecessor, was written as function composition, and it had no * either.

So + here is a choice rather than something inherited. Unlike the pipe, it can be given a meaning in R, Python and Julia. It commutes wherever order should not matter, and it leaves the atoms as nouns so that * can exist.

26.6 Summary

Each operator reads as a phrase when a plot is read aloud, and | and / have two readings each:

Operator Reads as Example
+ “…and also…” point + line
* “…derived by…” bar * bin
| “…split into panel columns by…” data(gm) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) | facet(continent)
/ “…split into panel rows by…” data(gm) + point + x(gdp) + y(life) / facet(era)
| “…beside…” (one specification) | (another)
/ “…above…” (one specification) / (another)

A finite set of rules, and more plots than you will ever need to draw.
